Three Papers on Number Machinery

What each one set out to do, what they actually ran, the mechanism each proposes written as steps you can execute by hand, and — kept separate on purpose — what each admits it could not show.

Every claim below carries a tag. shown means they measured it. claimed means they assert it on partial evidence. admitted means the authors themselves say they do not know. Learning to keep those three apart is most of what reading a paper well consists of.

One paper at a time

Language Models Use Trigonometry to Do Addition

GPT-J 6Bmain subject, simple MLPs
80.5%its accuracy on a+b
0–99operand range, single tokens
T = 2, 5, 10, 100the four periods

What they set out to do

Not to test whether models can add — that was known. To reverse-engineer the machinery: find what a number is inside the model, then find which parts turn two of them into a third.

What they actually ran

  1. Fed the integers 0–360 and looked at the residual stream after block 0. A Fourier transform showed sharp peaks at periods 2, 5 and 10; a PCA showed a straight line in the value.
  2. Proposed that both facts fit one shape — a helix — and fitted it by linear regression. Nine numbers per number.
  3. Tested whether the model uses it, by activation patching: run a clean prompt, run a corrupted one, paste the fitted helix in place of the real activation, and see if the right answer comes back. Compared against four baselines including PCA.
  4. Ranked every attention head and every MLP by how much patching it restored, then kept the smallest set that recovered most of the behaviour.
  5. Went down to individual neurons, fitted each one's firing pattern with a sum of the same four periods, and checked the fits by patching them back in.

The mechanism, as steps you can run by hand

THE CLOCK ALGORITHM          to compute   a + b =

  1  REPRESENT     on a's own token, build nine numbers from a:
                     [ a,
                       cos(2πa/2),   sin(2πa/2),
                       cos(2πa/5),   sin(2πa/5),
                       cos(2πa/10),  sin(2πa/10),
                       cos(2πa/100), sin(2πa/100) ]
                   multiply by a fixed matrix C  ->  helix(a)
                   same for b, on b's own token

  2  TRANSPORT     attention heads in blocks 9-14 copy
                   helix(a) and helix(b) onto the final "=" token

  3  COMBINE       MLPs 14-18 turn helix(a), helix(b) into helix(a+b)
                   THIS IS THE ADDITION. MECHANISM NOT IDENTIFIED.
                   conjectured:  cos(a+b) = cos a cos b - sin a sin b

  4  READ OUT      MLPs 19-27 read helix(a+b) and raise the logit
                   of the answer token

Try step 1 by hand for a=3 and a=63. On the period-10 circle both land in the same place; on the period-100 circle they do not. That is how four circles pin down one number.

What holds it up, and what does not

  • shown Deleting the helix directions from the residual stream hurts the model about as much as deleting the whole layer.
  • shown Nine fitted numbers beat a 27-dimensional PCA at reconstructing the last token's state.
  • shown About 1% of neurons, with the rest averaged away, still solve 80% of problems.
  • shown A helix fitted to a shuffled ordering of the numbers has no causal effect — so the shape is not merely flexible enough to fit anything.
  • admitted Step 3, the actual addition, was never isolated. The trigonometric identity in the title is a hypothesis. The same gap defeated the original Clock paper in 2023.
  • admitted The helix loses to a plain PCA baseline on three of their six tasks — so numbers carry structure the helix does not describe.
  • admitted The fit is markedly weaker on Llama 3.1, which they suspect is its gated MLPs. "The Clock could be one method of an ensemble."
  • admitted Models that split numbers into digits "must use additional algorithms to collate digit tokens." This sentence is the door we walked through.

A finding people skip, in Appendix F

When GPT-J is wrong, it is wrong by exactly −10 (45.7% of numeric errors) or +10 (27.9%). The obvious guess is a failed carry. They tested it with a chi-squared test and ruled it out. The cause is instead that the read-out from the helix is itself periodic with period 10, so neighbours ten apart are always half-promoted.

Language Models Learn Universal Representations of Numbers

8 models3 families, none above 30B
top-63frequencies: perfect agreement
99%previous digit, from the last token
up to 42%of division errors repaired

What they set out to do

Two things, and the second is the one that gives the paper its title. First, test whether the sinusoidal number representation is a quirk of one model or a law across models. Second, argue that general-purpose interpretability tools give wrong answers on numbers — and that the tool you choose changes the conclusion you reach.

What they actually ran

  1. Compared the number embeddings of eight models against each other directly, and against a control of 1,000 ordinary word pieces.
  2. Fourier-decomposed each model's number embeddings and asked whether different models rank the same frequencies highest. For the top 63, every pair of models agreed perfectly.
  3. Built a new probe that forces a sinusoidal shape and learns a frequency, amplitude and phase per feature. Fitted it at every layer of six models.
  4. Crucially, took the numbers from real sentences in four domains, not from synthetic sums — and showed probes trained on synthetic text fail to transfer, while probes trained on real text transfer both ways.
  5. Trained the probe on one layer and tested it on every other layer. Near-perfect transfer for all the middle layers.
  6. For multi-token numbers, read at the last token and asked the probe to recover the earlier pieces. Compared against TunedLens, an off-the-shelf tool.
  7. Finally intervened: nudged activations toward the shape the probe expects, on cases the model was getting wrong.

The method, as steps you can run by hand

THE param-sin PROBE       read a number out of an activation

     f(x)  =  Sᵀ · ( W_in · x )
     S     =  w_a · sin( w_φ + w_f · r )        r = 0, 1, ... 999

     learn per feature:   w_f  frequency
                          w_a  amplitude
                          w_φ  phase
     the sine shape is IMPOSED, not discovered

TO STUDY ANY MODEL

  1  collect activations for numbers sitting in real sentences
  2  fit one probe per layer;  hold out numbers it never sees
  3  accuracy of read-back  =  how sinusoidal AND how precise
  4  move the probe to another layer, or another model, unchanged
  5  multi-token number?  read at the LAST token,
                          ask it for the EARLIER pieces
  6  to intervene:  h' = α·h + (1-α)·e_target
                    where e_target is what the probe wants to see

What holds it up, and what does not

  • shown All eight models rank the same top-63 frequencies. The control word pieces do not behave this way, so it is specific to numbers.
  • shown A probe trained on one layer reads another layer at close to 100% for the middle of the network.
  • shown The preceding number piece is recoverable from the last token at 99%. Beyond three tokens it collapses toward zero.
  • shown TunedLens, the general-purpose tool, says the opposite — that digits cannot be recovered past two steps. The sinusoidal probe shows that was the tool's failure, not the model's.
  • shown Steering toward the expected shape repairs up to 30% of multiplication errors and 42% of division errors.
  • claimed That how well the model maintains the sinusoid determines its accuracy. The steering result supports this; it is stronger than what a probe alone licenses.
  • admitted Three families only, and nothing above 30B parameters, for want of hardware.
  • admitted English-centric. Number systems differ in Chinese and Japanese; untested.
  • admitted The probe has a blind spot: every error on days and months falls on the first item, and persists when the list is reversed.

The transferable lesson

Their §4.1 and our afternoon made the same point from opposite directions. A blunt instrument reports absence where a sharp one finds 99%. We saw it too: the cosine-similarity square made it look as though the first digit mattered more with depth, while a probe showed it was fully readable from block 1 the whole time. A negative result is a statement about your instrument until you have shown otherwise.

The Shape of Addition: Geometric Structures of Arithmetic

3 × 10 digitsthe sums they used
σ ≈ 0.05the model's fitted noise level
R² = 0.80fit of the error curve
86.3 → 89.6%accuracy after their fix

What they set out to do

Explain a specific failure rather than a capability. Big models do long addition and are reliably wrong by one. The question is why that error, and why it clusters where it does.

What they actually ran

  1. Ten thousand sums of three ten-digit numbers, greedy decoding, with a forced prefix so the model outputs digits and nothing else.
  2. Stopped generation at the first wrong digit, so every activation they study follows a fully correct history. That rules out errors caused by earlier errors — a control worth stealing.
  3. Projected the final-layer activations at one column position into two dimensions, with the ten output digit vectors included as fixed landmarks.
  4. Trained probes for six different quantities: the true digit, the model's digit, the column sum, the incoming carry, whether it was right, and a continuous quantity of their own devising.
  5. Found a direction between two clusters and pushed activations along it to see whether the output digit flips — the causal test.
  6. Built a corrector: read two quantities out of the activation and overrule the model when they disagree with what it said.

The mechanism, as steps you can run by hand

CARRY AS A NOISY MEASUREMENT      column p, units on the right

  THE ARITHMETIC (what is true)
      raw sum          r_p  = sum of the digits in this column
      carry potential  Φ_p  = Σ  r_(p+j) / 10^j      for j = 1, 2, 3...
                              a CONTINUOUS number, not 0 or 1
      true carry       c_p  = floor(Φ_p)
      correct digit    s_p  = (r_p + c_p) mod 10

  WHAT THEY SAY THE MODEL DOES
      perceived        Φ̂_p  = Φ_p + ε        ε ~ Normal(0, σ²),  σ ≈ 0.05
      its carry        ĉ_p  = floor(Φ̂_p)
      its digit        ŝ_p  = (r_p + ĉ_p) mod 10

  WHEN IT GOES WRONG
      only when Φ_p sits near a whole number, so a little noise
      tips it over the floor boundary

      P(error | Φ)  =  Q(δ/σ)  +  Q((1-δ)/σ)        δ = Φ mod 1

      -> error rate is a BATHTUB:
           worst   at Φ ≈ 1.0, 2.0, 3.0 ...
           safest  at Φ ≈ 1.5, 2.5, 3.5 ...

  WORKED EXAMPLE FROM THE PAPER
      columns to the right give  Φ = 1.4 + 0.11 = 1.51
      true carry = floor(1.51) = 1
      1.51 is far from a boundary  ->  safe

This is the one to do on your whiteboard. Pick a three-number sum, compute Φ for each column, and mark which columns sit near a whole number. Those are the digits the paper predicts it gets wrong.

What holds it up, and what does not

  • shown The predicted bathtub shape is there in the data, fitting at R²=0.80 and implying a noise level of about 0.05.
  • shown Probe accuracies line up with the geometry: the model's own digit reads at 98.8%, the true digit only 94.9% — the gap is exactly the errors, where the activation has physically drifted to the wrong cluster.
  • shown Pushing an activation along the carry direction flips the digit, and states near a boundary flip with a smaller push than stable ones.
  • shown The sharpest result in the paper is an ablation. Give the model its own column sum but the true carry and accuracy jumps to 96.0%; give it the true column sum but its own carry and it only reaches 90.5%. The carry is the bottleneck, not the addition.
  • claimed That the model literally holds a continuous carry and floors it. The evidence is a probe that recovers Φ at 92% plus a well-fitting error curve. Consistent with the story; not the only story consistent with it.
  • admitted One column position, and mainly the final layer. Layer-by-layer work is pushed to an appendix.
  • claimed The pictures come from UMAP, which is a non-linear projection. Distances in such a picture are not distances in the model. Their steering result is what rescues this, because it does not depend on the picture.

How the three fit together

The gaps, and which one is ours

Open, and big

Nobody has isolated the step that actually adds. Two papers, three years apart, both stop at the same wall. This is the real prize and it is not a weekend's work.

Open, and awkward

Does the helix survive in a model that never sees a whole number, only digits? Paper 1 says such models "must use additional algorithms" and leaves it. Paper 2 shows the pieces are recoverable but does not ask whether the four circles are still there. Nobody has fitted a helix to a digit-split model.

Open, narrow, and within reach today

Every model in all three papers uses classic attention in every block. Qwen3.5 and later run three linear-attention blocks for every one classic block — a different mechanism for the same job, verified from the config files and from the fact that the two model classes share no linear-attention code at all.

So: does any of this transfer to a hybrid, and does the mixer type change where a number gets assembled? The honest sizing is that this is a transfer test, not a discovery, and "yes, it transfers" would be a thin result. What makes it worth doing is that it is cheap, it is unclaimed, and the tools already exist.

How to read one of these

Not a general recipe. This is the order that actually worked on these three, reconstructed after the fact.

1
Find the question, not the titleTitles are advertisements. "Uses Trigonometry to Do Addition" describes the part they conjectured, not the part they demonstrated. Read the last paragraph of the introduction; the real question is usually there.
2
Go straight to the setup sectionWhich models, how big, how much data, what prompt. Everything downstream is conditional on this and it is where the limits are decided. Two of these three could not run a model above 30B.
3
Separate what was measured from what was proposedEvery paper mixes them, usually in the same paragraph. Tag each claim as shown, claimed or admitted. If you cannot tell which a sentence is, that is itself information.
4
Hunt for the controlsThe quality of a paper lives here. Kantamneni fitted a helix to shuffled numbers to prove the shape was not merely flexible. Wen stopped generation at the first error so no mistake could be blamed on an earlier one. A paper with no controls is a paper making a claim about its own instrument.
5
Read the limitations section first, if you are short of timeIt is where the authors tell you what they could not do, and it is almost always the most honest page. It is also where the next project is.
6
Then read the appendices nobody readsAppendix F of the first paper contains a dead hypothesis: the off-by-ten errors look exactly like a failed carry, and a statistical test rules that out. That negative result is more useful than several pages of the main text.
7
Write the mechanism as steps before you believe itIf you cannot turn the claim into something you could execute on paper, you have not understood it yet, and you cannot tell whether the paper has either.
